\begin{abstract}
\noindent\small Exchanges have listed derivatives on property prices since 1991 and none has become liquid. Event contracts on prediction markets are the latest attempt. This paper asks whether they remove the obstacles that stopped earlier property derivatives, using all real estate contracts listed on Kalshi since \firstOpen{} and the house price contracts on Polymarket. They do not. Kalshi's \reMarkets{} real estate contracts account for \reVolShare{} per cent of the volume in its economics category, and a real estate event trades about \condGap{} per cent less than a comparable economics event. On Polymarket the median account traded \walletMedianUsd{} dollars and \walletOneMonth{} per cent of accounts never returned. The contracts are quoted as tightly and priced as accurately as other economics contracts, so they work as forecasts, but they do not transfer risk. In \pairs{} repeat sales from Cook County, Illinois, an index hedge removes at most \hedgeUnderTwo{} per cent of house-level price variance for holdings under two years, and no real estate contract on Kalshi runs longer than \reMaxHorizonMonths{} months. The open order books would cover \depthMedianShare{} per cent of a hedge for a single 500,000 dollar home.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Housing is the largest asset most households own, and they cannot hedge it. Real estate as a whole is the largest asset class without a liquid derivatives market \citep{geltner2007pricing,fabozzi2020thirty}. \citet{case1993index} proposed cash-settled futures and options on house price indices to close that gap, and \citet{shiller1999home} proposed insurance built on them, but every attempt to list such contracts has failed for want of trade \citep{shiller2008derivatives}. Thirty years on, \citet{fabozzi2020thirty} name four obstacles: settlement indices that fit the contracts badly, negligible liquidity in a market where nearly everyone wants the same side, the lack of an accepted pricing model, and regulation.

Prediction markets now list contracts on the same statistics. A contract pays one dollar if, say, a house price index ends above a stated level, and nothing otherwise. It differs from a futures contract in two ways that matter here. The payoff is a claim on a state, so that a ladder of strikes traces out a whole distribution, and the buyer is someone with a phone and a brokerage app, not an institution with a clearing account \citep{diercks2026kalshi}. The hope is that speculators who enjoy taking a view on house prices will supply the liquidity that hedgers never supplied to each other, speculators being what makes a futures market deep enough for hedgers to use \citep{pagano1989trading}. This paper tests that hope on Kalshi,\footnote{\url{https://kalshi.com}. Market data come from the exchange's public API at \url{https://api.elections.kalshi.com/trade-api/v2}.} which \citet{diercks2026kalshi} describe as the largest federally regulated prediction market, and on Polymarket.\footnote{\url{https://polymarket.com}. Event and trade data come from \url{https://gamma-api.polymarket.com} and \url{https://data-api.polymarket.com}.}

The contracts barely trade. All house price contracts listed on Kalshi since \firstOpen{} traded \priceVolM{} million one-dollar contracts (Table~\ref{tab:inventory}), whereas the CME housing futures that are usually described as a failure traded 612 million dollars of notional in their first eighteen months \citep{shiller2008derivatives}, about \cmeRatio{} times as much. A real estate event trades roughly \condGap{} per cent less than an economics event with the same listing period and number of strikes (Table~\ref{tab:regression}). Polymarket's public trade records show who takes part, which is mostly accounts that trade a few dollars once (Table~\ref{tab:wallets}).

I do not think this is a slow start that time will fix. The contracts are good forecasts, quoted on most days at the same spreads as other economics contracts and as well calibrated (Table~\ref{tab:quotes}), which is what the literature on prediction markets would lead one to expect \citep{wolfers2004prediction}. What they cannot do is hedge. Repeat sales in Cook County show that an index-settled contract removes almost none of an owner's price risk at holding periods under two years (Figure~\ref{fig:hedging}), in line with \citet{giacoletti2021idiosyncratic} and \citet{eichholtz2021total}, and neither exchange lists anything longer. The order books, finally, are too thin for a single household (Table~\ref{tab:depth}). The obstacles are the horizon, the basis and the missing counterparty, and neither a new payoff nor a better app does anything about those.

\section{Thirty years of property derivatives}

The record is set out by \citet{shiller2008derivatives} and \citet{fabozzi2020thirty}, and the facts in this section come from those two accounts unless another source is given. The Chicago Board of Trade considered a house price future in 1990 and dropped the idea after a survey in 1993 showed plenty of interest in protection against falling prices and little in selling it. The London Futures and Options Exchange launched property futures in May 1991 and terminated them in October, after little trade and a wash-trading scandal. Goldman Sachs listed covered warrants on UK house price indices in 2003, and a year later the open interest was roughly what would hedge a hundred houses. The CME launched futures and options on the S\&P/Case-Shiller indices in May 2006; notional traded reached 612 million dollars by November 2007, open interest peaked at 109 million, and the market survives only in diminished form. Commercial property did somewhat better in the UK, where swaps on the IPD index traded more than three billion pounds of notional in the first quarter of 2007, than in the US, where no single index commanded the same acceptance \citep{geltner2007pricing}.

Event contracts on housing are not new either. HedgeStreet offered consumers ten-dollar binary bets on house price indices for six cities in 2004 and closed for lack of trade, and UK spread-betting firms offered bets on house prices from 2001, which were seen as gambling and traded sparsely \citep{shiller2008derivatives,fabozzi2020thirty}. \citet{gurkaynak2006macroeconomic} study auctions of digital options on macroeconomic data releases that began in 2002 and find the prices well calibrated, and \citet{carlton1984futures} mentions a proposal for futures on housing starts in the early 1980s. What is new is how many people trade event contracts.

The literature offers three main explanations for the thin markets. The first is the settlement index: repeat-sales indices are revised substantially and systematically downward as new sales arrive \citep{clapham2006revisiting}, which is awkward for a contract that must settle on one published number. The second is basis risk, because a homeowner owns one house and not the index. \citet{dejong2008hedging} find that access to city-level housing futures improves a homeowner's welfare only slightly for that reason, \citet{giacoletti2021idiosyncratic} shows that idiosyncratic risk accounts for a large fraction of house price variance, a fraction that falls as the holding period lengthens, and \citet{eichholtz2021total} show that for total returns the idiosyncratic part still makes up about half of risk at holding periods of fifteen years, a growing share of it coming from rental yields that are persistent at the level of the property. The third is the missing counterparty, since almost every household and lender is naturally long. Demand may also be weaker than the gains estimated by \citet{englund2002hedging} suggest: \citet{sinai2005owner} point out that owning a home is itself a hedge against rent risk, and \citet{caplin2003home} report that take-up of subsidised home equity insurance in Illinois municipalities was 15 per cent at best.

\section{Event contracts as claims on states}

Let $H_T$ be the value of a house price index published at date $T$. An event contract with strike $K_j$ pays $\mathbf{1}(H_T > K_j)$. With strikes $K_1 < \dots < K_n$, the difference between neighbouring prices is the price of a claim on the interval,
\begin{equation}
  q_j - q_{j+1} = \text{price of } \mathbf{1}(K_j < H_T \le K_{j+1}),
\end{equation}
and a homeowner who wants protection against a fall below $K$ can approximate a put by buying the complements of the contracts with strikes below $K$, so that a dense ladder spans payoffs that a single futures contract does not \citep{ross1976options,breeden1978prices}. Under conditions set out by \citet{wolfers2006interpreting} the prices can be read as the market's probabilities, although \citet{manski2006interpreting} shows that with heterogeneous beliefs a price only bounds the mean belief of traders.

Each strike is a separate order book, however. Splitting trade across $n$ strikes does not by itself make a hedge dearer, because a hedger who replicates a linear position over a given range of the index buys $n$ contracts of one $n$-th the size, so that for a given spread the cost depends on the range covered and not on the number of steps. It costs more only if spreads widen or depth shrinks because each book receives a fraction of the order flow, which is what the model of \citet{pagano1989trading} predicts, in which depth depends on who else is expected to trade and trade tends to concentrate in one market. New contracts usually die in any case. \citet{carlton1984futures} finds that the majority of the futures markets listed in the United States between 1921 and 1983 failed within ten years, and among the features he names as necessary for success housing lacks one: prices that are highly correlated across the slightly different specifications and locations of the product.

The other obstacles stay where they were. The contract still settles on an index, the homeowner still owns one house, and the horizon moves in the wrong direction, because a household's exposure runs for years whereas event contracts resolve on the next data release. If the idiosyncratic share of house price variance is largest at short holding periods \citep{giacoletti2021idiosyncratic,eichholtz2021total}, an index contract hedges least at exactly the horizons that prediction markets list.

\section{Data}

The data are all markets in the economics category of Kalshi's public API, downloaded on \dataDate{}:\footnote{All code for this project is available at \url{https://github.com/thies/prediction-markets-real-estate}.} \nMarkets{} markets in \nSeries{} series and \nEvents{} events, the first of which opened in \firstOpen{}. A series is a recurring question, an event is one settlement date of that series, and a market is one strike within an event; volume is the number of one-dollar contracts traded over the life of the market.

I identify real estate series by hand from their titles and settlement sources and sort them into five groups: house prices (FHFA, Zillow and Canadian indices), rents (rent indices, CPI shelter, owners' equivalent rent), the 30-year mortgage rate, housing activity (sales, starts, permits, inventory), and credit and commercial real estate (mortgage and CRE delinquency, Manhattan office vacancy). Table~\ref{tab:series} in the appendix lists every series. Everything else in the category forms the comparison group, which is dominated by Federal Reserve decisions, inflation, payrolls and petrol prices. Commercial property is nearly absent: Kalshi lists no contract on a commercial price, rent or return index, and the \adjSeries{} series on data centre construction, data centre capacity and hotel room demand are very limited in scope and size, with \adjMarkets{} markets and \adjVol{} contracts between them, and stay in the comparison group.

The regressions use closed events, for which volume is final: \regEvents{} events in \regSeries{} series, of which \regReEvents{} are real estate events (Table~\ref{tab:summary} in the appendix). For Polymarket I use the \polyEvents{} house price events listed since \polyLaunch{}, which settle on the Parcl Labs price index for the United States and \polyMetros{} metropolitan areas, together with the record of every trade in them. Polymarket's other real estate contracts, \polyMortgageEvents{} events on the mortgage rate since 2022 and \polyOtherEvents{} others, are too few to analyse.

\subsection{Quotes and price accuracy}

Volume does not say what it would have cost to trade or whether the prices were any good. Daily closing quotes are available for every real estate market listed for at least two days and for a fixed sample of about a quarter of the other economics markets, which leaves \quoteMarkets{} settled markets, \quoteReMarkets{} of them real estate (Tables~\ref{tab:quotes_summary} and~\ref{tab:quotesgroup} in the appendix). The median quoted spread is \spreadComparison{}, and with controls the two groups do not differ significantly (Table~\ref{tab:quotes}, column 1). Real estate markets have a two-sided quote on \twoSidedRe{} per cent of days against \twoSidedOther{} per cent elsewhere (column 2), which fits the account of \citet{diercks2026kalshi}, who note that Kalshi is supported by professional market makers. What is missing is anyone to take the other side: house price markets trade on \priceDaysTraded{} per cent of days and rent markets on \rentDaysTraded{} per cent, against \otherDaysTraded{} per cent in the comparison group. Nor is a spread of \spreadRe{} cents cheap, since on a contract priced at 50 cents it amounts to a round trip of \spreadRoundTrip{} per cent.

Prices are about as accurate as elsewhere. Taking the quote midpoint one day before close as the forecast, the raw Brier score is worse for real estate, \brierRe{} against \brierOther{}, but real estate prices sit closer to 50 cents, where any forecast scores worse, and conditional on $p(1-p)$ the difference is \brierDiff{} and insignificant, both one day and seven days out (columns 3 and 4). A regression of outcomes on prices gives a slope of \calSlope{} with no difference for real estate (column 5; Figure~\ref{fig:calibration} in the appendix), which matches what \citet{diercks2026kalshi} find for Kalshi's macroeconomic contracts. Of the four benefits that \citet{fabozzi2020thirty} list for property derivatives, information about future prices is thus the only one delivered, and even that rests on only \priceForecastN{} house price markets with a one-day forecast.

\subsection{Hedging capacity of an index contract}

Suppose the contracts were liquid. To see how much of an owner's price risk they could then remove, I use the Cook County Assessor's parcel sales \citep{cookcounty2026sales}. After dropping sales before 2000, multi-parcel sales, sales below 10,000 dollars, non-warranty deeds and flagged duplicates, consecutive sales of the same house or condominium at least six months apart give \pairs{} repeat-sale pairs on \pairProperties{} properties. For each pair, the log price change of the house is regressed on the payoff of a hedge written on the S\&P/Case-Shiller Chicago index \citep{spcaseshiller2026} over the same months, and the $R^2$ is the share of house-level variance the hedge removes, the measure that \citet{bertus2008hedging} apply to Las Vegas.

Figure~\ref{fig:hedging} gives the result by holding period (Table~\ref{tab:hedging} in the appendix). For owners who sell within two years the index removes at most \hedgeUnderTwo{} per cent of the variance of their price change, and the share then rises to \hedgeTwoThree{} per cent at two to three years, \hedgeThreeFive{} per cent at three to five and \hedgeFiveTen{} per cent at five to ten. This is the pattern that \citet{giacoletti2021idiosyncratic} documents for California and \citet{eichholtz2021total} for Amsterdam, and it is the opposite of what the exchanges supply, the longest house price contract on Polymarket running \polyMaxHorizon{} days. The payoff shape matters much less than the horizon: a ladder of seven binary contracts, the median number of strikes per house price event on Kalshi, removes as much variance as a linear position at every holding period, as the spanning argument of \citet{ross1976options} implies.

The larger shares come from one episode, however. For houses bought in 2000--2011, whose holding periods span the boom and the bust, the index removes \earlyThreeFive{} per cent of variance at three to five years, but for houses bought from 2012 on only \lateThreeFive{} per cent, and in New York City, where the public sales file \citep{nyc2026sales} starts in 2016, at most \nycMaxR{} per cent at any holding period (Tables~\ref{tab:hedgingperiod} and~\ref{tab:hedgingrobust} in the appendix). An index hedge protects against a market-wide crash and does little for the owner of one house in ordinary years. The shares are also upper bounds, since the hedge matches the holding period exactly, hedge ratios are fitted in sample and the index is taken as final, and they concern price risk only, whereas \citet{eichholtz2021total} show that rental yields account for a growing part of property-level risk at longer horizons. Short holding periods are a selected sample, heavy with renovated resales and distressed sales, so the result below two years should be read as no evidence of hedgeable risk and not as a precise zero.

\subsection{Who trades}

Polymarket's \polyEvents{} house price events traded \polyPriceVolM{} million contracts up to \dataMonth{}, for which takers paid \polyTakerUsdM{} million dollars. That is more than Kalshi's house price contracts and still a rounding error against the CME figure. The first monthly events, opened in January 2026, traded a median of \polyJanMedian{} contracts, those opened from March to June \polyMarJunMedian{}, and the quarterly contracts that replaced them in July \polyJulyMedian{} (Table~\ref{tab:cohorts} in the appendix). I cannot say how much of the July jump is organic, because the exchange pays liquidity rewards on open contracts and does not report whether closed ones carried them.

Because Polymarket settles on a public blockchain, each trade carries the wallet addresses of both sides. A wallet is an account and not a person, but the addresses show what kind of participation there is. The house price markets saw \polyTx{} transactions among \polyWallets{} wallets. The median wallet traded \walletMedianUsd{} dollars in total, \walletUnderHundred{} per cent traded less than 100 dollars, and \walletOneMonth{} per cent appear in the contracts of a single launch month and never again. Table~\ref{tab:wallets} sorts wallets by the share of their dollars that came from resting orders and by the ratio of their net position to their gross trading: \mmWallets{} wallets with \mmShare{} per cent of the dollars provide liquidity and \holdWallets{} wallets with \holdShare{} per cent take a position and hold it. The \bigWallets{} wallets above 10,000 dollars are generalists. The median one has traded about \bigMedianMarkets{} markets across the exchange and \bigRewarded{} of them have collected more than 1,000 dollars in liquidity rewards (Table~\ref{tab:topwallets} in the appendix), so that for them a house price contract is one more market to quote or one more bet. The largest traded \topWalletUsd{} dollars. These are not the pension funds, insurers and hedge funds that \citet{fabozzi2020thirty} expected to carry the other side of a property derivatives market.

\subsection{A household's hedge}\label{sec:household}

There is little sign that households use these contracts to hedge. A household hedging its home would trade the contracts for one area, come back each time they are relisted, keep what it buys, and stay on one side of the market's consensus. Of the \hedgerCandidates{} wallets that traded at least 1,000 dollars in two or more launch months, \hedgerOneArea{} concentrate four fifths of their trading on one area, of which \hedgerLike{} also kept a one-sided position, the largest having traded about \hedgerLikeUsd{} dollars.

Nor could the markets absorb a real hedge. An owner of a 500,000 dollar home who wants cover against a 10 per cent fall needs contracts that pay 50,000 dollars. Table~\ref{tab:depth} summarises the order books of all open house price contracts on \depthDate{}. Adding up, across the strikes of an event, the contracts on offer within five cents of the best quote gives a median of \depthKalshiMedian{} dollars of payout per event on Kalshi and \depthPolyMedian{} on Polymarket, or \depthMedianShare{} per cent of what this one household needs, and the deepest event offers \depthMaxShare{} per cent. Prices would move long before that: an order for 100 contracts fills within five cents on \fillNearHundred{} per cent of book sides, an order for 1,000 on \fillNearThousand{} per cent, and the 7,000 contracts per strike that a seven-strike ladder would take on \fillNearLadder{} per cent (Table~\ref{tab:impact} in the appendix). A single homeowner would exhaust the quotes of a metropolitan market, and would have to do so again every few months as contracts expire. The snapshot is of one day, and market makers may refill their quotes, but there is no sign of standing capacity on the scale of even a handful of houses.

\section{Conclusion}

Prediction markets have not given real estate the derivatives market it lacks. Event contracts on house prices, rents and mortgage rates are listed, quoted and accurately priced, and almost nobody trades them. They make up \reVolShare{} per cent of the volume in Kalshi's economics category, the accounts that trade them on Polymarket are small, do not return and include no one who looks like a hedger, and the open order books could not carry the hedge of a single home. What the contracts supply is a forecast of the next index release, which is useful and is not risk transfer.

The reasons are the old ones. \citet{fabozzi2020thirty} put liquidity and the missing counterparty at the centre, and the retail speculators who were supposed to fill that gap have come to the exchanges and trade the Federal Reserve and the price of petrol instead. Had they come to housing, the contracts would still not hedge much, because at the horizons listed an index removes almost none of the price risk of an individual house, and in calm markets it removes little at any horizon. A market that completes real estate would need contracts that run for years, on indices local enough to track a house, with counterparties willing to hold the other side for that long. Nothing in the event contract format rules that out, since a ladder of binary contracts hedges as well as a future, but nothing in the present markets points towards it either.

Several limits should be kept in mind. The hedging test uses Case-Shiller indices and not the Zillow and Parcl indices on which the event contracts settle, covers two cities, and fits hedge ratios in sample. Order book depth is observed on one day, since neither exchange archives its books, although the code behind this paper records a snapshot at every update, so that a history will build up. Polymarket's quotes and price accuracy have not been analysed, and for the CME contracts after 2007 I rely on \citet{fabozzi2020thirty}. These markets are young and changing, and the analysis is built to be rerun as they do.
